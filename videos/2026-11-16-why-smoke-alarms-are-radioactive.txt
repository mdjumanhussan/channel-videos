Title:
The Radioactive Speck Inside Many Smoke Alarms

Description:
Many smoke alarms hold a tiny, sealed speck of radioactive americium, and it is there on purpose. This short animated explainer shows how that speck lets the alarm notice smoke, why it is safe in normal use, and how the other kind of alarm uses light instead.

What is covered:
- The speck: americium-241 in a metal cup the size of a pencil eraser
- Alpha particles: they travel only a few inches, and paper stops them
- The chamber: two charged plates, ions, and a tiny steady current
- Why smoke makes the current drop and sets off the alarm
- Quick question: is it dangerous? (no, in normal use; never take one apart)
- Photoelectric alarms: smoke scatters a light beam onto a sensor
- Which kind notices flaming and smouldering fires first (NIST tests)
- What Fire and Rescue NSW recommends

The alarm, chamber and particles are simplified drawings, and the dose bars are illustrative.

Why is my smoke alarm radioactive? #Shorts

Description:
A speck of americium turns the air in a small chamber into ions so a tiny current flows; smoke blocks the ions, the current drops, and the alarm sounds.
